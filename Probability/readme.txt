Probability

The Probability system is the component of Jim Rockies responsible for estimating the probability of an underlying proposition. Rather than beginning with a prediction or a statistical formula, the system begins with the proposition itself and attempts to determine what would have to be true for that proposition to occur.

A proposition is a statement about an observable outcome. For example, a player prop such as "Jackson Chourio records two or more total bases" is a proposition that must either become true or remain false. The system identifies what the proposition describes, what action is involved, what outcome is required, and what conditions surround that action.

The purpose of this process is to move from the sentence describing an outcome to the reality represented by that sentence. Probability is not the starting point. It is the result of investigating the conditions under which an outcome occurs.

Information Requirements

The system is built around three requirements for information: it must be qualitative, attributable, and measurable.

Qualitative means the thing must be identifiable and nameable. If the system refers to a defensive scheme like drop coverage, there must be an identifiable basketball action that the term describes. If it refers to a pitcher, there must be an identifiable characteristic of that pitcher. The system does not work with abstractions that cannot be described. The thing comes first.

Attributable means the information must be connected to its source. An observation should be linked to the player, team, opponent, action, condition, or game that produced it. Without attribution, a measurement loses its meaning because the system cannot determine what reality produced it.

Measurable means the identified thing can be represented numerically. If a thing cannot be counted, compared, or analyzed quantitatively, it cannot be treated as quantitative information. The system does not invent numbers for convenience.

These requirements establish a boundary around the system. Jim Rockies does not compress reality into a single number merely because that number is easier to use. The system must remain capable of returning from the number to the thing that produced it.

Data and Evidence

The system distinguishes between data and useful evidence. A statistic can accurately describe something that happened while providing little information about the current proposition. A player's past performance is a legitimate observation, but it can be strongly affected by opponent quality, opportunity, health, role, game state, and other conditions.

The system evaluates not only whether an observation occurred, but also the conditions under which it occurred and whether those conditions are relevant to the current proposition. Every piece of information must prove itself to be relevant. The existence of data is not sufficient reason to use it.

The system asks:

· Does this information describe something about the proposition?
· Can we identify what it describes?
· Can we attribute it?
· Can we measure it?
· Can we establish a relationship between it and the outcome?

If it can, it may become evidence. If it cannot, the system does not pretend to know more than it actually does.

Relationships and Frequency

The system is particularly concerned with relationships between conditions and outcomes. If a condition changes, the system examines whether the frequency of an outcome also changes. If a relationship is sufficiently consistent and relevant, it becomes evidence used in the probability calculation.

Frequency is an important component. An observed frequency represents how often an outcome has occurred under a defined set of conditions. A high frequency does not guarantee the next occurrence, but it provides evidence about the underlying probability when the observations are sufficiently relevant, comparable, and stable.

The system looks at reality game by game when that information is available. It does not treat the dataset as one undifferentiated mass of numbers. Individual observations retain their conditions and relationships.

For example, a player may perform well against one type of defensive coverage and poorly against another. The relevant question is not simply whether the player is "good." The relevant question is what the player actually does under the conditions represented by the proposition.

If the proposition is Jackson Chourio recording two or more total bases, the system examines the player, his opportunities, the opposing pitcher, the pitcher's characteristics, handedness, ballpark, lineup position, expected playing time, and other identifiable conditions. Each condition must earn its place through demonstrated relevance. The objective is to cover the relevant bases, not to collect every available statistic.

Limits of Knowledge

The system distinguishes between an observation and an inference from that observation. A single game may provide an accurate observation while providing weak evidence about future performance. A larger body of comparable observations may provide stronger evidence. The question is not whether the past is useful, but what information the available observations actually provide.

The future itself is not directly observable. Jim Rockies does not claim information it does not possess. There are things that can happen during a game that may materially affect an outcome but cannot be known from the available information. If the system does not have evidence of that information, it does not pretend to know it. The system works from the information that can actually be established.

This is an important limitation. Watching a game can reveal information that a dataset does not contain. The objective is to determine what can be known from the available information and to quantify it as accurately as possible.

All information exists in the present and is derived from observations, measurements, relationships, and forecasts available at the time the proposition is evaluated. The problem is not past versus future. The problem is ignorance versus information. The system cannot observe the future directly. It can only use present knowledge to estimate the distribution of possible future outcomes.

The system does not attempt to eliminate randomness. Randomness exists in the underlying events being modeled. The objective is to eliminate as much ignorance as possible. There is a difference between something being unknowable and something being unknown because we have failed to investigate available information. Jim Rockies attempts to remove the second.

Decomposition and Conditional Probability

For a player prop, the system decomposes the proposition into the actions and opportunities that can produce the required result. A total-bases proposition can depend upon the number of plate appearances, the probability of reaching base, the type of hit produced, the opposing pitcher, pitch selection, handedness, ballpark, weather, lineup position, and other conditions. These are not merely variables in a model. They are descriptions of things that happen in reality. The system identifies those things first and then determines how they can be measured and related to the outcome.

Probability is treated as conditional rather than universal. The probability of an outcome depends upon the information available about the conditions surrounding it. The system accounts for uncertainty in the quality of its own evidence. Sample size, comparability, context, opportunity, health, opponent strength, role, and game conditions affect how much weight an observation should receive.

The objective is not to find one number that explains everything. The objective is to construct a collection of measurable relationships that describe the proposition well enough to produce a defensible distribution of outcomes.

Conceptual Progression

The conceptual progression of the system is:

Proposition → Decomposition → Qualitative Identification → Attribution → Measurement → Conditions → Available Data → Data Quality → Relevance → Relationships → Observed Frequency → Conditional Distribution → Probability

Comparison with Market Price

The resulting probability can be compared with the probability implied by the market price. This creates two separate quantities within Jim Rockies:

1. The probability implied by the market price.
2. The probability estimated by the Probability system from the underlying information.

The difference between these quantities provides the basis for expected-value analysis. The market price determines the payoff available from a position. The probability model determines the estimated likelihood of the underlying outcome. These quantities are evaluated independently before being combined for expected-value analysis.

This distinction applies to player props and other markets where there is no simple binary game-level outcome. The system is not limited to determining which team will win a game. It determines the probability of propositions concerning players, teams, events, statistics, and other measurable outcomes.

Implementation

The system can eventually represent these relationships as structured data. Observations can be associated with players, teams, opponents, actions, conditions, outcomes, timestamps, and other identifiers. This allows the system to search historical observations according to the conditions relevant to a current proposition.

The initial implementation begins with granular observations and mathematical functions before introducing more advanced predictive methods. The system first establishes the underlying relationships between propositions, conditions, observations, frequencies, and outcomes.

The purpose of the first stage is to create a research and measurement system capable of taking a proposition, investigating the relevant information, constructing an outcome distribution, and producing a probability that can be evaluated against the market price.

The system can later incorporate additional statistical models, player and team characteristics, game-state variables, projections, injuries, lineups, opponent-specific information, and other forms of information as their relevance is established.

Central Principle

The central principle of the Probability system is:

Name the thing. Attribute the thing. Measure the thing. Investigate the thing. Then assign probability to what follows from it.

Jim Rockies therefore does not begin by pretending to know the future. It begins with what can actually be known. Our model identifies the proposition, decomposes it into observable realities, measures those realities, examines their relationships and frequencies, accounts for uncertainty, and constructs a probability from the evidence. The system does not attempt to know what it cannot know. It attempts to remove ignorance from what can be known.
